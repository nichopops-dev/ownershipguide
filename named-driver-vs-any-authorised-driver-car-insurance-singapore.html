Match the Certificate to Every Real Driver

A car-insurance quote is incomplete until every person who may use the car has been tested against it. “Named driver,” “authorised driver,” and “unnamed driver” can carry product-specific meanings, restrictions, and excesses.

Do not treat “any authorised driver” as universal permission. The actual driver may still need the owner’s permission, a valid licence, minimum age or experience, an allowed use, and compliance with every other policy condition.

Decision rule

Build the driver list from real life, including backup use. Then ask the insurer to state in writing whether each person is covered and the total excess that would apply.

Use names, ages, licence dates, and actual uses when requesting an answer. A generic question about “family members” can hide the condition that later matters.

The GIA consumer guide tells motorists to check the certificate to determine who is authorised to drive. Do that before looking at the premium difference.

Price the backup-driver scenario

As a dated product example, Income’s current Drivo information states a S$600 basic excess unless another amount is shown. It states an additional S$2,500 for an unnamed driver under 27 or with less than one year of driving experience, and S$500 for an unnamed driver aged 27 or older with at least one year of experience.

Now assume a hypothetical backup driver is age 25 with three years of driving experience. If the quoted terms apply and the excesses are cumulative, an applicable claim could leave this cash amount:

ComponentAmount
Basic excessS$600
Additional unnamed-driver excessS$2,500
Total retained for the tested claimS$3,100

Suppose naming that driver instead costs a hypothetical S$180 more a year. Three years of additional premium would be S$540. That is S$2,560 less than the illustrated S$3,100 claim outlay, although naming the driver does not guarantee that every extra excess disappears.

The calculation is a comparison prompt, not a quote. Adding a driver changes underwriting and may change the premium, cover, excesses, or acceptance. Get the endorsement and revised schedule before the person drives.

Make a policy change complete

  1. Send the insurer the driver’s identity, age, licence date, driving history, and actual use.
  2. Ask whether the insurer accepts the driver and from what date and time.
  3. Ask for the revised premium and every applicable excess.
  4. Receive the endorsement or amended schedule.
  5. Check that the name, effective date, uses, and amounts are correct.

Keep the final certificate, schedule, wording, and endorsements together. Use the excess comparison to test each driver’s claim-month cash need, then normalise the quotes.

FAQ

Does any authorised driver mean anyone can drive my car?

No. The driver must still satisfy the certificate and policy conditions, which may cover permission, licensing, age, experience, use, and other restrictions. Read the actual contract.

Can an unnamed driver still be covered?

Some policies cover an unnamed driver subject to their terms and additional excesses; other products restrict cover to declared drivers. Confirm the exact product and obtain written confirmation.

Can I add a named driver during the policy period?

Ask the insurer. If it agrees, obtain the endorsement and confirm the revised premium and excess before that person drives. A request alone does not amend the contract.

What should I record for each household driver?

Record whether the person is declared and permitted, their age and driving experience, the uses allowed, and the full applicable excess if they make a claim.
